# Grammatical Voice & Gender Bias in Crash Blame Attribution 

An experimental study investigating whether grammatical voice (active vs. passive) interacts with driver gender to affect blame attribution in motorsport crash scenarios. 

## Background 

Hart (2018) found no significant effect of grammatical voice alone on blame attribution, but speculated voice might interact with socially salient identity characteristics. This study tests that unexplored hypothesis by crossing voice with driver gender in a controlled experiment. 

## Research Questions & Hypotheses 

**H1:** Grammatical voice alone will not affect blame attribution. 
**H2:** Female drivers will be rated as less skilled and less successful than male drivers at baseline. 
**H3:** Female drivers will receive higher blame ratings, amplified when combined with passive voice. 

## Design
2x2 between-subjects factorial experiment:
**Voice:** Active versus Passive
**Driver Gender:** Male versus Female 

55 participants rated driver responsability, control, deliberation, recklessness and suggested penalty severity on a 7-point likert scale. 

## Key Findings
There was no significant effect of voice alone on blame attribution (consistent with Hart, 2018). 

There was also no significant gender bias in basline skill/success ratings. This affected H3, as no effects were found. 

## Tools & Methods 
'R' 'ggplot2' 'independent samples t-test' 'factorial ANOVA' 'likert scale analysis' 'experimental linguistics'

## Theoretical Frameworks 
- Systemic functional lingusitics (Halliday, 1978)
- Critical discourse analysis (Van Dijk, 1993)
- Stereotype threat (Steele, 1997)

## Ethics & Data Privacy
Participant data is fully anonymised. Participants were assigned random numerical 
identifiers. The study received ethical approval through the University 
of Warwick.
